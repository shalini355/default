The problem

Exams, placements and deadlines pile up, and reflecting on how you feel shouldn’t feel like filling in a medical form. Three gaps shaped the design: clinical language, no clear next step after venting, and sign-up friction before anyone can try it. These are design hypotheses from my brief, not findings from user research.

What I designed

  • Hinglish-friendly chat with quick-reply chips so users can respond in one tap.
  • Anonymous start so there is no account wall.
  • Mood log with intensity and tags that turns into a short summary.
  • Support screen with Tele-MANAS, iCall and 112 as one-tap call buttons.
  • Calm fallback copy when the AI is unavailable, offering journaling or breathing instead of a raw error.

Principles I followed

  • Warm, not clinical: plain, conversational language.
  • Small steps: one or two actions, never a long list.
  • Honest limits: KAI says what it can’t do.

What’s next

I’ve drafted a KAI 3.0 direction: a daily check-in, an explainable score and a stronger safety flow. It is proposed, not built or tested. First step is usability sessions with 5 to 8 students on the core check-in.

Scope note: KAI is a student prototype, not a therapist, diagnostic tool or emergency service. It hasn’t had formal user research or clinical review.

The problem

Beginners need to choose what to practise and see how they did. Unclear settings, inconsistent question layouts and thin answer feedback made practice confusing.

Decisions I made

  • Configure before starting: language, difficulty and category sit in one clear setup step.
  • Handle missing content: explanations show when available, with a useful fallback when they aren’t.

What I learned

Know which data fields are required or optional before building components. It prevents layout bugs and rework, and it showed me why designers and developers should talk early.

What I’d improve today

  • A shared colour, type and component system across screens.
  • Better contrast and keyboard focus states.
  • A results screen that links answer review to what to practise next.

How I decide what to design

No fixed framework. Four questions I ask before I open Figma, and again before I hand off.

What is confusing?

Before drawing rectangles in Figma, I write down what the user is trying to do and where they might hesitate, get lost or give up.

What if it fails?

Happy paths are easy. I map edge cases early: empty results, slow responses and an accidental tap on Back.

Is it clear in 3 seconds?

I check hierarchy: what catches the eye first and whether the main button is unmistakable.

Does it hold in code?

As a CS student I check layout shifts, heavy queries and mobile rendering while I design.
